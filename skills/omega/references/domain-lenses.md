# Domain lenses

Read only the sections relevant to the request. Use these as examples of consequential dimensions, not a fixed interview order. Start from the person's intention and existing knowledge. Describe observable outcomes before technical labels.

## Images, illustration, and graphic design

Explore purpose and placement, subject and action, mood, visual treatment, composition, light and color, important details, exclusions, and output constraints. Prioritize usage, subject, and emotional intent before minor stylistic choices.

Translate vocabulary through effects:

| Everyday choice | Possible technical interpretation |
| --- | --- |
| "I want to see the place around the person" | A wider composition with contextual background |
| "The face should stand out; the rest should melt away" | Subject separation and shallow depth of field |
| "Soft, translucent washes, with paper showing through" | Watercolor-like treatment |
| "Thick, visible strokes and textured paint" | An impasto-like treatment |
| "Flat, opaque areas of color" | A gouache-like or graphic treatment, depending on examples |

Do not present style labels as precise guarantees; ask what the user likes in a reference. A reference may communicate color, framing, texture, or mood independently.

For rain, offer perceptual contrasts, not an exhaustive vocabulary lesson:

- Fine drizzle softening the distance: a restrained, misty atmosphere.
- Steady rain with visible droplets and wet reflections: an unmistakably rainy scene.
- Wind-driven downpour with diagonal streaks and spray: movement and intensity.

Treat those emotional associations as suggestions, not universal facts. Distinguish rain amount, wind, droplet visibility, lighting, and the subject's reaction. Do not infer thunder, sadness, darkness, or night just because rain was requested. Do not repeat unverified claims about how many words a language has for rain.

Reveal hidden production needs when relevant: room for text, legibility at the actual viewing size, accurate product labels, necessary source assets, transparent background, cropping, and the printer's supplied requirements. Do not invent brand assets or insist on print questions for a casual digital image.

Example first round for an undefined rainy illustration:

> What should we see in the scene? Should the rain feel gentle and quiet, clearly present, or powerful and wind-driven? Is this for a phone wallpaper, a printed image, or something else?

Adapt the next round to the answers; do not immediately ask every composition and lighting question.

## Applications, websites, and automation

Explore the user's real workflow before choosing a technology. Ask for an ordinary situation: who starts, what they need to do, what information enters, and what a successful end looks like.

Then examine relevant dimensions:

- Single person or multiple roles; who may read or change which information.
- Devices, shared use, accessibility, and whether the app needs to work without internet.
- Information to store, sensitivity, retention, export, deletion, and recovery.
- Integrations, payments, notifications, repeated tasks, and external dependencies.
- Failure cases: lost connection, duplicate action, wrong entry, unavailable service.
- Budget, deployment, ongoing maintenance, ownership, and support.

Explain architecture only when it helps a decision:

- **Interface/front end:** screens and controls the person interacts with.
- **Server/backend:** behind-the-scenes logic that can share information and enforce access rules.
- **Database:** organized persistent information.

Do not force this architecture on a project that does not need it. Do not ask a novice to choose frameworks, authentication protocols, or database engines without a concrete reason.

Own basic implementation safeguards appropriate to the actual system: enforce access where data is served, protect secrets, validate input, use appropriate authentication, and plan recovery. Ask who should have access, not whether private data should be protected. Never imply a frontend-only login screen secures backend data.

For payments, clarify the business flow and recommend a suitable hosted/provider-managed approach when applicable. Avoid collecting or storing payment credentials in discovery. Verify provider details before promising support or pricing.

Separate a demonstration with fictitious data from a production system. Explain what remains to verify before real people depend on it.

## Video and audio

Explore audience, platform, duration, message, emotional rhythm, footage or assets, voice, sound, text, and delivery needs. Respect existing constraints such as no music without repeatedly reopening them.

Translate "dynamic" into possible visible changes: faster cuts, animated typography, subject movement, camera movement, or stronger sound cues. Offer contrasts because these are different effects.

Reveal relevant constraints: captions and readability, crop-safe framing, voice intelligibility, asset permissions, pacing, and platform specifications. Verify changing platform requirements when needed. Do not promise rights to an asset simply because it is accessible online.

## Writing, communication, and learning

Explore recipient, purpose, intended response, essential facts, tone, length, format, and examples the user likes or dislikes. Ask what a sample achieves rather than just its genre label.

Distinguish supplied facts from claims needing verification. Preserve the author's intended meaning. Do not manufacture testimonials, quotations, qualifications, or evidence to make a brief more persuasive.

For learning, identify the task the person wants to perform and use examples to gauge understanding without calling them a beginner. Explain one needed concept at a time and apply it immediately.

## Business and consequential decisions

Explore the decision, alternatives, audience or customer, existing evidence, success measure, resources, reversibility, and downside. Separate assumptions to test from facts already known.

Do not ask the user to supply a conversion rate, market size, legal requirement, or financial return as if these were personal preferences. Research relevant facts or state what would be needed to estimate them.

For regulated or high-stakes matters, clarify only context needed for a useful, appropriately bounded answer. Do not request identifying records when an anonymized summary is sufficient.

## Domains not listed

Build a tailored lens from the goal, domain deliverables, common failure modes, adjacent disciplines, and user constraints. Consult reliable references for gaps. Say when expertise or tools cannot support a conclusion. Keep the discovery workflow; do not invent specialist certainty.
